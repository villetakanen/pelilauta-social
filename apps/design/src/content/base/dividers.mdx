An `<hr>` marks a thematic break: one passage has ended and the next begins. Pelilauta
renders a writer's `---` as one, so the break someone typed is the break a reader gets.

The rule ships in the design system stylesheet, which every application imports. There
is nothing to apply — the element carries both the meaning and the mark.

<DsComposition themes caption="A thematic break between two passages, in Light and Dark">
  <p>Kaukasalon kartta on nyt ladattavissa kahtena versiona.</p>
  <hr />
  <p>Seuraava passage alkaa tästä.</p>
</DsComposition>

```html
<p>One passage.</p>
<hr />
<p>The next.</p>
```

## The mark carries no space

A divider states no margin of its own. The space above and below it is the rhythm of
the content area holding it, the same interval that area puts between any two of its
children — see [Content containers](/base/content-containers).

This is where the rule departs from Cyan and from v20, which both gave `hr` a
`--cn-line` margin. Those systems had no rhythm for an area to state, so the element
had to carry its own; here it would be counted three times.

## What it is made of

A hairline border on the block-start edge, in the `--cn-color-border` role — the
structural boundary, not a text or a surface colour.

The hairline is one device pixel, not a reader-scaled length. Almost everything in the
system is stated in units that grow with the reader's text size; a divider is one of the
few things that should not, because it marks a boundary rather than carrying text, and
it holds the same weight at any size.

A divider spans the width it is given and asks for none.

## Where it does not go

Dividers publishes the element and nothing else. A component that draws the same
hairline inside its own box — a card edge, a rail's top rule — states that itself, and
answers for it in its own book.
