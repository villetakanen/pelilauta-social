# Colour theory and perception foundations for design-system colour discipline

Research notes with sources. Five questions, findings grouped below, open caveats at the end.

---

## 1. Perceptual uniformity — OKLCH/OKLab over HSL/sRGB

**What "perceptual uniformity" means.** A space is perceptually uniform when equal numeric steps correspond to equal perceived differences, and when its three axes (lightness, chroma, hue) behave as independent, "orthogonal" dimensions — moving one shouldn't smear the others. That's the property HSL and raw sRGB lack and Oklab/Oklch were built to have.
Source: Björn Ottosson, ["A perceptual color space for image processing"](https://bottosson.github.io/posts/oklab/) (2020).

**Why HSL/sRGB fail.** HSL's "lightness" is a naive transform of RGB channels, not a perceptual quantity: in HSL, pure yellow, cyan and magenta read as much lighter than pure red or blue at the same "L", so a scale built by walking HSL's L axis produces visibly uneven steps and inconsistent contrast at a given lightness value depending on hue. CIE Lab/LCH improve on this but still mispredict hue, particularly for blues (a known artifact of the underlying von Kries-style chromatic adaptation math). Ottosson built Oklab specifically to fix the blue hue-linearity failure in Lab/LCH and the hue-dependent lightness failure in HSV/sRGB, evaluating candidate spaces (CIELAB, CIELUV, IPT, CAM16-UCS, etc.) against each other's known weaknesses.
Sources: Ottosson, ["A perceptual color space for image processing"](https://bottosson.github.io/posts/oklab/); [Smashing Magazine interview with Ottosson](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2024/10/interview-bjorn-ottosson-creator-oklab-color-space/) (2024); [Oklab, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklab_color_space).

**Practical payoff for building scales.** Because L, C, H are (approximately) independent in Oklab/Oklch, a design system can hold hue and chroma constant and step only L to build a tint/shade ramp that looks evenly spaced, or hold L constant across hues to build a set of same-weight semantic colours (e.g. status colours that read as equally prominent). This is the mechanical reason design tokens are increasingly authored in OKLCH rather than HSL.

**What OKLCH lightness does *not* guarantee about WCAG contrast — the approximation and where it breaks.**
- OKLCH's L is a much better proxy for *perceived* lightness than sRGB or HSL lightness, and in practice the contrast between two colours is overwhelmingly driven by the gap in L — chroma differences have comparatively little effect and hue differences are secondary. A commonly cited rule of thumb: roughly a 40% (0.40) gap in OKLCH L is enough to clear WCAG 2's minimum text-contrast thresholds for most hue/chroma combinations.
  Source: [oklch-skill accessibility-contrast notes](https://github.com/jakubkrehel/oklch-skill/blob/main/skills/oklch-skill/accessibility-contrast.md).
- That correlation is not identity. WCAG 2's contrast formula is computed from relative luminance (a photometric quantity derived from linear-light sRGB), not from any perceptual lightness model — it is a different number space than OKLCH's L, so "same ΔL → same WCAG ratio" holds only approximately, and the mapping is known to be nonlinear and hue/chroma-dependent at the edges. Practitioners' own tooling flags the OKLCH-lightness-gap heuristic as an approximation requiring verification against the actual WCAG ratio, not a substitute for computing it.
  Source: [oklch-skill accessibility-contrast.md](https://github.com/jakubkrehel/oklch-skill/blob/main/skills/oklch-skill/accessibility-contrast.md); [Lea Verou, "On compliance vs readability: Generating text colors with CSS"](https://lea.verou.me/blog/2024/contrast-color/).
- The deeper break is that WCAG 2's contrast math itself is not perceptually uniform, so no lightness model — OKLCH included — can be patched onto it to yield reliably correct predictions across the full range. WCAG 2's ratio badly overstates contrast for dark-on-dark pairs (a 4.5:1 pass can be functionally unreadable near black), and its treatment of mid-tones is skewed: 18% grey is numerically "middle" but is not perceptually middle-contrast. This is the documented rationale for APCA (Accessible Perceptual Contrast Algorithm), built by Andrew Somers/Myndex from perceptual research rather than from the WCAG 2 luminance ratio, which produces a signed Lc value that is comparable across the tonal range (an Lc 60 pair reads with roughly the same legibility whether it sits near white or near black) — something WCAG 2's ratio cannot promise. APCA is a closer conceptual partner to OKLCH (both are grounded in perceptual lightness) than WCAG 2 is, but APCA is *not itself* the current legal/normative WCAG standard.
  Sources: [Myndex/SAPC-APCA, "Why APCA as a New Contrast Method?"](https://git.apcacontrast.com/documentation/WhyAPCA.html); [Datawrapper, "It's time for a more sophisticated color contrast check for data visualizations"](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/color-contrast-check-data-vis-wcag-apca); [WCAG 2 vs APCA discussion, Myndex/SAPC-APCA GitHub #30](https://github.com/Myndex/SAPC-APCA/discussions/30).

**Bottom line for a design system:** OKLCH lightness is a good *authoring* heuristic for building visually even scales and a reasonable first-pass predictor of contrast, but it is not a certified substitute for running the actual WCAG (or APCA) contrast formula on the resolved colour pair — especially near the extremes of the tonal range or with unusually high-chroma colours, where the correlation between ΔL and the contrast metric weakens.

## 2. Why limited palettes cohere — restraint as identity and legibility

**Itten vs Albers — two different arguments for the same discipline.** Both were Bauhaus colour-course authors whose plates look superficially similar but whose aims diverge: Johannes Itten argued for objective rules of colour *harmony* — that specific combinations (his colour star, the twelve-hue wheel, complementary/triadic schemes) produce psychologically and symbolically "correct" results, so a designer restrains the palette to the combinations the theory sanctions. Josef Albers argued the opposite premise but reached the same practical discipline from it: colour perception is *relative*, not absolute — "a color is almost never seen as it physically is," because context (adjacent colours, area, background) changes what the eye reports. Since no colour can be trusted to read the same way twice, Albers's discipline is to work with a small, controlled vocabulary of colours long enough to learn how each behaves in combination, rather than trusting an expanding, uncontrolled set.
Sources: ["Bauhaus Color Theory: Itten, Kandinsky, Albers and Klee"](https://www.color-meanings.com/bauhaus-color-theory-itten-kandinsky-albers-klee/); [Academia.edu, "A Comparison between Itten's and Albers' Bauhaus Colour Theories"](https://www.academia.edu/41530207/A_Comparison_between_Ittens_and_Albers_Bauhaus_Colour_Theories); Josef Albers, *Interaction of Color* (Yale University Press, 1963); [Artforum on Albers's *Interaction of Color*](https://www.artforum.com/columns/josef-alberss-interaction-of-color-193938/).

**Why restraint produces coherent identity and legibility, in both accounts:**
- Itten's route: a palette limited to a harmonic scheme is legible because the relationships between its colours are pre-validated — the eye doesn't have to resolve an arbitrary, unrehearsed combination.
- Albers's route: a palette limited to a small, known set is legible because the *designer* has tested how each colour actually reads against every other colour in the set (simultaneous contrast distorts colour unpredictably — see §4) — an uncontrolled, large palette multiplies the untested pairs combinatorially, and each new pair is a new place perception can go wrong.
- The print/editorial tradition (masthead colour, a fixed small set of section/rubric colours) applies the same logic operationally: a reader learns a small, stable set of colour cues once and then reuses that learning issue after issue; an editorial palette that changes wholesale each time forces the reader to re-learn the code, which is the opposite of what a colour system is for. This is the same mechanism Sharp's distinctive-assets argument formalizes for brand colour (§5) — repetition of a *narrow* set is what lets the cue become a recognized code at all.

## 3. Colour as signal — why status/semaphore colours must stay conventional

**Origin of the convention, and why it's a convention rather than a law of perception.** The red/green stop/go pairing is not perceptually inevitable; it is a specific historical artifact. Railway signalling in the 19th century originally used red for "danger/stop", green for "caution" and clear/white for "go" — white-for-go was abandoned after white lantern-glass failures caused fatal misreadings, and green was promoted to "go" as a safer fallback, with a distinct colour (yellow/amber) later inserted for "caution." John Peake Knight's 1868 semaphore proposal for London road traffic imported the railway red/green scheme directly. The convention that stabilized (red=stop, green=go, amber=caution) survived because of accumulated infrastructure and law, not because red is inherently a "stop" colour to the eye.
Sources: [Colorsift, "History of Traffic Signal Colors: Red & Green"](https://colorsift.com/articles/history-of-traffic-signal-colors-red-green); [Today I Found Out, "The Origin of the Green, Yellow, and Red Color Scheme for Traffic Lights"](https://www.todayifoundout.com/index.php/2012/03/the-origin-of-the-green-yellow-and-red-color-scheme-for-traffic-lights/).

**Why this argues for stability, not for "expressive" reuse of status colours.** Because the mapping is a learned, socially agreed sign (a semiotic convention) rather than a perceptual universal, its entire value depends on staying fixed: a signal system only works if the audience can rely on the code without re-deriving it each time. A design system that reassigns red to something other than error/danger, or greens/oranges inconsistently across surfaces, breaks the one thing semaphore colour is for — instant, low-cognition recognition — and forces the reader back into interpretation.
Source: [Herzing University, "Semiotics and Cultural Competence"](https://www.herzing.edu/blog/semiotics-and-cultural-competence) (on signs as convention-dependent, not intrinsic).

**Cross-cultural caveat.** The red=danger/stop=green=go convention is robust mainly because it's now backed by nearly universal transport infrastructure, but the underlying *symbolic* associations of the same hues are not universal and sometimes point the opposite way: red signifies prosperity and celebration in Chinese culture, is associated with mourning in parts of Southern Africa, and reads as danger/passion/warning in much of the West; green can signify luck, nature, or (in some contexts) can carry negative associations. A status-colour system exported globally (as this product's audience spans Finnish/Swedish/English readers, and potentially wider) should treat the traffic-light-style red/amber/green mapping as safe *because of its transport-signal precedent*, not assume every hue choice carries the same meaning everywhere.
Sources: [Winsome Marketing, "The Semiotics of Color: Cultural Coding in Global Brand Strategy"](https://winsomemarketing.com/winsome-marketing/the-semiotics-of-color-cultural-coding-in-global-brand-strategy); [Buzzword Creative, "Colour Meanings | Colour Semiotics & Their Cultural Meanings"](https://buzzwordcreative.co.uk/colour-semiotics-and-what-they-mean-in-other-cultures/).

## 4. Simultaneous contrast — why fixed role pairings beat ad-hoc combination

**The phenomenon.** Michel-Eugène Chevreul, chemist and director of dyeing at the Gobelins tapestry works, formulated the "law of simultaneous contrast" in the early-to-mid 19th century after investigating complaints that dyed yarns looked wrong once woven next to other colours (the dye was fine; adjacency was the problem): two colours placed side by side each shift in perceived hue, value and saturation, pushed apart from each other — their differences are exaggerated and their similarities suppressed. The eye does not report a colour patch's "true" value; it reports a value relative to what's around it.
Sources: [Wikipedia, "Simultaneous Contrasts"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneous_Contrasts); [Oberlin College Libraries, "Check It Out: Color Theory: Chevreul & Albers"](https://libraries.oberlin.edu/news/2024/02/21/check-it-out-color-theory-chevreul-albers).

**Albers turned the observation into a design method.** *Interaction of Color* (1963) opens on the claim that "in visual perception a color is almost never seen as it really is," and demonstrates it directly: an identical swatch reads as a different colour (and different lightness) depending on what surrounds it — his standard exercise places one colour on multiple backgrounds and asks the student to record what they actually perceive, not what they know to be physically true.
Sources: [Yale University Press, *Interaction of Color*](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300179354/interaction-of-color/); [Artforum on Albers](https://www.artforum.com/columns/josef-alberss-interaction-of-color-193938/); [Medium, "Interaction of Color: The Importance of Joseph Albers to Color Theory"](https://medium.com/@straubkira/interaction-of-color-the-importance-of-joseph-albers-to-color-theory-575b6d3fad10).

**Why this argues for fixed role pairings (e.g. a token pair "text-on-surface") rather than free combination.** If a colour's appearance — including its effective *contrast* — depends on what it sits against, then a token's contrast and legibility properties are not a property of that colour alone; they are a property of the *pair*. Validating one colour against one background does not license using that same colour freely against every other background in the system: the same nominal "text" colour can under- or over-perform depending on the surface it lands on, exactly because simultaneous contrast will shift its apparent lightness/chroma differently against each one. A design system that defines and tests specific role pairs (this text token on this surface token) rather than a loose palette anyone can recombine is applying Chevreul/Albers's finding directly: only the tested pair carries a guarantee; an ad-hoc pair is an unrun experiment.

## 5. Brand identity theory — one identity hue over many

**Distinctive Brand Assets (DBA).** The term (and the research programme) is Byron Sharp and Jenni Romaniuk's, out of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute for Marketing Science (Sharp, *How Brands Grow*, 2010; Romaniuk's later distinctiveness research operationalizes it). A distinctive brand asset is any non-verbal cue — colour, logo, shape, character, sound, packaging — that has, through *consistent, repeated* use, become uniquely and immediately attributable to one brand, ahead of any competitor.
Sources: [Umbrex, "Distinctive Brand Assets Framework (Ehrenberg-Bass)"](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/marketing-frameworks/distinctive-brand-assets-framework-ehrenberg-bass/); [Distinctive BAT, "Distinctive Brand Assets"](https://www.distinctivebat.com/distinctive-brand-assets/); [Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, "The distinctive asset in the room"](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/the-distinctive-asset-in-the-room).

**Why one hue outperforms many.** The mechanism the theory relies on is *mental availability* — the likelihood a brand is noticed, recalled and considered in a buying/using moment, which rises with how easily a cue is attributed to the brand at a glance, without conscious thought. That attribution speed is a direct function of *consistency*: a colour used the same way, in the same role, across every touchpoint for years, becomes a fast, low-effort cue (Coca-Cola red, Tiffany blue, McDonald's golden arches, T-Mobile magenta are the standard examples cited across this literature). A brand that instead rotates through several "brand" colours, or uses the identity hue interchangeably with others in the same visual role, dilutes the cue: the eye has no single, fast association to latch onto, and each colour individually accrues weaker recognition than one colour used consistently would. Distinctive-asset research reports that brands with strong, consistently-deployed assets measure materially more salient than competitors relying on weaker or more variable cues (cited figure: roughly 52% more salient on average).
Sources: [Your Marketing Rules, "Building Distinctive Brand Assets"](https://www.yourmarketingrules.com/building-distinctive-brand-assets/); [Umbrex, "Distinctive Brand Assets Framework"](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/marketing-frameworks/distinctive-brand-assets-framework-ehrenberg-bass/); [Brand Genetics, "How Brands Grow [Speed Summary]"](https://brandgenetics.com/how-brands-grow-speed-summary/).

**Relation to §2 and §4.** This is the same restraint argument as Itten/Albers and the same fixed-pairing argument as Chevreul, applied to brand rather than to a single composition: identity accrues to a *narrow, stable* code (one hue, used the same way, everywhere) precisely because recognition is a function of repetition against a stable reference, and a design system's "one identity/accent hue" rule is the DBA argument transposed into token architecture.

## Open caveats

- **OKLCH ≠ contrast-checked.** The strongest caveat across the research: nothing in Oklab/Oklch's construction, nor in APCA, changes the fact that an actual accessibility claim needs the contrast formula run on the final resolved colour values. OKLCH lightness is an authoring aid and a decent predictor, not a certificate.
- **APCA is not (yet) the enforceable standard.** APCA is the more perceptually faithful metric and pairs well conceptually with OKLCH, but WCAG 2.x's ratio remains the normative/legal baseline most audits check against as of this research; the two can disagree, sometimes sharply, especially in dark-mode and near-black/near-white pairs.
- **Itten and Albers are not one theory.** They're frequently cited together because their teaching plates look alike, but their justificatory claims are incompatible (Itten: colour harmony is objectively knowable and rule-governed; Albers: colour perception is inherently relative and rule-resistant, hence the *need* for constant testing). Both converge on "restrain and test the palette," but a design-system rationale should say which argument it's making, since they support slightly different practices (Itten licenses precomputed "harmonious" combinations as safe by construction; Albers does not — it insists every pairing still be checked).
- **Traffic-signal colour is convention-plus-infrastructure, not perceptual law.** The red/green mapping is robust mainly because of transport-law entrenchment, not because red and green are intrinsically "stop" and "go" to human vision — a caution worth keeping distinct from the (accurate) claim that red/amber/green is now a safe, near-universal choice for status colour in software.
- **The DBA literature is drawn primarily from consumer-goods branding research (packaged goods, FMCG, retail).** Its empirical base (Ehrenberg-Bass) is strongest for mass-market brand recognition; applying it to a community-platform or design-system accent colour is an analogy, not a direct empirical claim about that context.
